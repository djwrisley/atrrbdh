title: "Learning Outcomes"
---

By the end of the workshop, participants will be able to:

1. Explain the role of automatic text recognition (ATR) in transforming digitized cultural heritage materials into searchable, discoverable, and computationally tractable texts.
2. Evaluate the suitability of different sources and ATR approaches according to material features, language, script, period, research goals, available infrastructure, and intended audiences.
3. Design and carry out a basic ATR workflow, including image preparation, layout analysis, segmentation, baseline detection, model selection, transcription, and post-correction, using platforms such as eScriptorium and Transkribus. 
4. Understand how ATR models are created and improved, including the roles of ground truth, transcription guidelines, model training, fine-tuning, and evaluation.
5. Compare different approaches to automated transcription, including general and bespoke HTR/OCR models and multimodal/LLM-based transcription, and critically assess their strengths, limitations, and biases.
6. Explore how ATR-created texts can support approaches such as text analysis, annotation, mapping, digital editions, and other forms of computational inquiry. 
7. Assess how text quality and post-processing choices affect downstream computational analysis and interpretation, rather than treating transcription as a neutral or finished product.
8. Critically analyze ATR as a scholarly, technological, and institutional practice, and how issues such as infrastructure, platforms, language and script coverage, bias, and human intervention shape what cultural heritage becomes machine-readable
9. Appreciate how ATR does not simply translate the past, but actively shapes what can count as data, what becomes visible or recoverable, and ultimately the future of cultural memory.
